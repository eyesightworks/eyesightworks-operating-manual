# 2. Executive Summary

Operating Governance defines how EyesightWorks Technologies organizes authority, accountability, decision-making, review, execution, change management, risk control, and operational coordination.

The purpose of governance is not to create unnecessary bureaucracy.

Its purpose is to ensure that:

* important decisions have clear ownership
* work remains aligned with company strategy
* execution can be reviewed objectively
* risks are visible
* standards are followed consistently
* exceptions are controlled
* changes are documented
* responsibilities are clear
* outcomes can be measured
* the company can grow without losing operational clarity

EyesightWorks follows the principle:

> **Clear Ownership → Evidence → Decision → Execution → Measurement → Review → Improvement**

Governance connects company strategy with day-to-day execution.

# 5. Governance Philosophy

EyesightWorks should remain fast-moving while maintaining sufficient control.

Governance therefore follows the following principles.

## 5.1 Governance Should Enable Execution

Governance exists to help the company make better decisions and execute them reliably.

It should not create unnecessary approval steps.

---

## 5.2 Ownership Must Be Clear

Every important responsibility should have an identifiable owner.

No significant business, product, technical, security, or operational responsibility should exist without ownership.

---

## 5.3 Decisions Should Be Evidence-Based

Important decisions should use available evidence.

Evidence may include:

* customer feedback
* user behavior
* market research
* financial information
* operational data
* engineering analysis
* technical constraints
* experiments
* performance metrics
* documented risks
* launch results

Assumptions should be identified as assumptions.

---

## 5.4 Governance Must Support Speed

Small and reversible decisions should move quickly.

Large, expensive, risky, or difficult-to-reverse decisions require stronger review.

Governance intensity should therefore increase with decision impact.

---

## 5.5 Documentation Is Part of Governance

Important decisions, changes, risks, exceptions, and approvals should be documented.

Documentation creates organizational memory and makes decisions traceable.

---

## 5.6 Accountability Must Be Measurable

Ownership means responsibility for both work and outcomes.

Where practical, owners should be accountable for measurable results rather than activity volume alone.

# 6. Governance Principles

EyesightWorks governance follows these principles:

### Principle 1 — Company First

Decisions should consider the long-term interests of EyesightWorks rather than isolated short-term convenience.

### Principle 2 — Business Before Code

Engineering work should support a validated business, customer, or operational objective.

### Principle 3 — Evidence Before Assumptions

Important decisions should use evidence whenever evidence is available.

### Principle 4 — Clear Ownership

Every significant responsibility should have an owner.

### Principle 5 — Appropriate Authority

Decision authority should match the importance and risk of the decision.

### Principle 6 — Reversible Decisions Should Move Quickly

Low-risk reversible decisions should not be unnecessarily delayed.

### Principle 7 — High-Impact Decisions Require Review

Major strategic, financial, legal, security, architectural, product, or customer-impacting decisions require appropriate review.

### Principle 8 — Decisions Must Be Traceable

Important decisions should have enough context to understand why they were made.

### Principle 9 — Standards Are Defaults, Not Obstacles

Standards should create consistency while allowing controlled exceptions when justified.

### Principle 10 — Learn and Improve

Governance should evolve based on evidence and operating experience.

# 10. Decision Authority

Not every decision requires the same level of approval.

EyesightWorks uses four decision classes.

## Class A — Routine Decisions

Examples:

* minor implementation choices
* routine maintenance
* ordinary content updates
* low-risk operational changes
* small reversible improvements

These should normally be made by the person responsible for the work.

---

## Class B — Important Operational Decisions

Examples:

* process changes
* moderate product changes
* dependency changes
* moderate customer-impacting changes
* changes affecting multiple functions

These require review by the relevant owner.

---

## Class C — Major Decisions

Examples:

* major product direction
* significant financial commitments
* major architecture changes
* significant security decisions
* major customer commitments
* major vendor changes
* significant business model changes

These require leadership or designated senior review.

---

## Class D — Strategic Decisions

Examples:

* entering a major new market
* changing company direction
* major acquisitions
* major investment decisions
* major organizational restructuring
* discontinuing a core business line

These require company-level strategic decision-making.

---

# 11. Decision Escalation

A decision should be escalated when:

* authority is unclear
* the impact exceeds the owner's authority
* the decision affects multiple teams
* there is significant financial risk
* there is significant legal or security risk
* the decision may significantly affect customers
* the decision is difficult to reverse
* stakeholders cannot reach agreement
* evidence is insufficient for a high-impact decision

Escalation should provide clarity rather than simply transfer responsibility.

# 18. Exception Management

Standards and policies may occasionally require exceptions.

Exceptions should not become informal permanent rules.

An exception should identify:

* the standard being bypassed
* reason for the exception
* owner
* risk
* duration
* mitigation
* approval
* review date

Temporary exceptions should have an expiration or review point.

---

# 19. Exception Principle

The default should be:

> **Follow the standard unless there is a documented reason to deviate.**

Exceptions should improve outcomes rather than simply avoid discipline or inconvenience.

Repeated exceptions may indicate that the underlying standard needs review.

# 21. Risk Escalation

A risk should be escalated when:

* potential impact is high
* likelihood is increasing
* mitigation is not working
* ownership is unclear

# 39. Performance and Accountability

Accountability should focus on outcomes rather than activity alone.

For example:

Instead of:

> "Engineering completed many tasks."

Measure:

> "The required product capability was delivered and validated."

Instead of:

> "Marketing published many posts."

Measure:

> "Marketing generated measurable customer engagement or qualified opportunities."

The company should value useful outcomes, validated learning, reliability, and measurable progress rather than activity volume alone.

# 40. Governance Failure Indicators

Governance may be failing when:

* nobody knows who owns a decision
* decisions are repeatedly reversed without learning
* risks remain hidden
* priorities change without explanation
* teams work toward conflicting goals
* important decisions are undocumented
* exceptions become the normal process
* meetings produce no decisions or actions
* metrics are collected but ignored
* customer evidence is repeatedly ignored
* technical work becomes disconnected from business objectives
* responsibilities overlap without clear authority
* important changes occur without appropriate review

These signals should trigger governance review.

# 41. Governance Improvement

Governance should itself be improved continuously.

When a governance failure occurs, ask:

### What happened?

### Why did it happen?

### Which governance control failed?

### Was ownership unclear?

### Was evidence missing?

### Was decision authority unclear?

### Was the process too slow?

### Was the process too weak?

### Did the failure create a risk?

### What should change?

Improvements should be recorded where appropriate.
