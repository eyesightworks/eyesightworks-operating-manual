# Business Architecture

## Purpose

This document translates the Company Vision into a practical, evidence-driven business model.

It defines how EyesightWorks Technologies intends to:

* create customer value
* identify customers
* solve customer problems
* deliver products and services
* generate revenue
* acquire customers
* retain customers
* validate business assumptions
* prioritize opportunities
* measure business performance

This document sits between company strategy and product design.

The operating sequence is:

**Vision → Business → Product → Architecture → Engineering**

---

# Executive Summary

EyesightWorks Technologies exists to help businesses across Africa operate better using practical software and AI.

The company creates value by solving meaningful operational problems through:

* business software
* industry platforms
* automation
* artificial intelligence
* digital marketplaces
* enterprise management systems

The company follows a **depth before breadth** strategy.

The business should therefore:

1. identify a specific customer problem
2. define the target customer
3. validate the problem
4. define a practical value proposition
5. test willingness to use or pay
6. build the smallest useful solution
7. measure results
8. improve based on evidence
9. expand only when justified

Business Architecture exists to prevent product and engineering decisions from being made without sufficient understanding of the business problem.

The company should prefer understandable business models before introducing unnecessary complexity.

## Product and Business Must Remain Connected

A product feature should have a clear relationship to:

* customer value

* strategic alignment
* measurable outcomes

---

## Validation Before Scaling

EyesightWorks should not scale an unvalidated business model merely because the technology works.

# Beachhead Market

Each product should identify one initial beachhead market.

A beachhead market should have:

* a clearly defined customer type
* a specific problem
* identifiable decision-makers
* a reachable customer population
* evidence of demand

* a reasonable path to payment

# Customer Problem Definition

Before developing a major product, define:

### Customer

Who experiences the problem?

### Problem

What problem do they experience?

### Current Solution

How do they solve it today?

### Pain

What makes the existing solution inadequate?

### Frequency

How often does the problem occur?

### Impact

What does the problem cost the customer?

### Desired Outcome

What would a successful solution change?

### Willingness to Pay

Would the customer pay to solve the problem?

# MVP Philosophy

The Minimum Viable Product should be the smallest solution capable of testing the most important business assumption.

An MVP should not be:

* the smallest number of features possible without value
* a low-quality product
* a technology demo with no customer purpose

The MVP should be:

> **The smallest useful product that produces meaningful evidence.**

# Decision Gates

Major product investment should pass through appropriate decision gates.

## Gate 1 — Problem

Is there sufficient evidence that the problem exists?

## Gate 2 — Customer

Is the target customer clearly understood?

## Gate 3 — Solution

Does the proposed solution appear to address the problem?

## Gate 4 — Payment

Is there evidence of willingness to pay?

## Gate 5 — Retention

Do customers continue receiving value?

## Gate 6 — Scale

Is there enough evidence to justify additional investment?

Not every product will require identical gates, but the underlying questions should remain.

# Business Architecture Quality Standard

A strong Business Architecture should make it possible to answer:

1. Who is the customer?
2. What problem are we solving?
3. Why does the problem matter?
4. How does the customer solve it today?
5. Why would the customer choose us?
6. What outcome do we create?
7. How do we reach the customer?
8. How do we make money?
9. Why would the customer continue paying?
10. What assumptions remain uncertain?
11. How will we validate them?
12. What evidence would justify further investment?

If these questions cannot be answered, significant product investment may be premature.
