# 2. Executive Summary

The Risk Register is the central repository for identifying, assessing, monitoring, mitigating, and reviewing significant risks affecting EyesightWorks Technologies.

Risk management exists to help the company:

* identify threats early
* reduce avoidable losses
* protect customers
* protect company resources
* protect product and infrastructure reliability
* improve decision-making
* prepare for uncertainty
* assign clear risk ownership
* monitor changes in risk exposure
* reduce surprises

The purpose of the Risk Register is not to eliminate every risk.

Risk is unavoidable in business, product development, technology, and innovation.

The objective is to ensure that important risks are:

> **Visible → Owned → Assessed → Mitigated → Monitored → Reviewed**

# 5. Risk Philosophy

EyesightWorks recognizes that uncertainty is a normal part of building a company.

The company therefore follows these principles:

### Risk should be visible

Hidden risks are harder to manage.

### Risk should have ownership

A risk without an owner is unlikely to receive sustained attention.

### Prevention is preferred where practical

Preventing a problem is generally preferable to responding after damage occurs.

### Not all risks require elimination

Some risks may be acceptable when the expected value justifies them.

### Risk should be proportional

High-impact risks require more attention than low-impact risks.

### Risk should influence decisions

Significant risks should be considered before major investments and commitments.

### Risk changes over time

A risk that is low today may become high later.

# 9. Risk Statement Format

A useful risk statement should describe:

> **Because of [cause], [event] may occur, resulting in [impact].**

Example:

> Because the primary infrastructure provider is a single point of dependency, a provider outage may occur, resulting in temporary product unavailability.

This format helps separate the cause, event, and consequence.

---

# 10. Risk Scoring

EyesightWorks may use a simple likelihood × impact model.

```text
Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact
```

Both factors are scored from 1 to 5.

### Likelihood

| Score | Description   |
| ----- | ------------- |
| 1     | Very Unlikely |
| 2     | Unlikely      |
| 3     | Possible      |
| 4     | Likely        |
| 5     | Very Likely   |

### Impact

| Score | Description |
| ----- | ----------- |
| 1     | Minimal     |
| 2     | Low         |
| 3     | Moderate    |
| 4     | High        |
| 5     | Severe      |

Maximum score:

**25**

---

# 11. Risk Levels

| Score | Level     | Response                           |
| ----: | --------- | ---------------------------------- |
|   1–4 | Low       | Monitor                            |
|   5–9 | Moderate  | Mitigate and monitor               |
| 10–14 | High      | Active mitigation and owner review |
| 15–19 | Very High | Leadership attention required      |
| 20–25 | Critical  | Immediate review and action        |

These thresholds are a governance tool, not a substitute for judgment.

A lower-scoring risk may still require attention when it involves security, legal obligations, customer safety, or other sensitive areas.

# 12. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk is the level of exposure before considering mitigation or existing controls.

Example:

> A public-facing API containing sensitive business operations has inherent security risk because it is exposed to external traffic.

Inherent risk helps the company understand the underlying exposure.

---

# 13. Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk that remains after controls and mitigation are applied.

```text id="5s7vc0"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Mitigation
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

The residual risk is often the more useful number for ongoing governance.

---

# 14. Risk Acceptance

Some risks may be accepted when:

* the risk is understood
* the potential value justifies the exposure
* mitigation would be disproportionately expensive
* the residual risk is considered manageable
* the appropriate decision authority accepts it

Risk acceptance should be intentional.

A risk should not be considered accepted merely because nobody acted on it.

---

# 15. Risk Treatment Options

EyesightWorks may respond to a risk through one or more strategies.

## 15.1 Avoid

Change the plan to remove the risk.

Example:

Do not launch a feature until a major security dependency is resolved.

---

## 15.2 Reduce

Lower the likelihood or impact.

Example:

Add validation, monitoring, backups, or access controls.

---

## 15.3 Transfer

Move part of the exposure to another party where appropriate.

Examples may include:

* insurance
* contractual arrangements
* specialist services

---

## 15.4 Accept

Proceed while consciously accepting the remaining risk.

Acceptance should be documented for significant risks.

---

# 16. Mitigation vs Contingency

These concepts should not be confused.

### Mitigation

Actions taken to reduce the chance or impact of the risk.

### Contingency

Actions taken after the risk occurs.

Example:

**Risk:** Database outage.

**Mitigation:**

* backups
* monitoring
* tested recovery procedures

**Contingency:**

* restore database
* communicate service status
* investigate failure
* activate recovery process

# 18. Risk Status

Recommended statuses are:

### Identified

Risk has been recorded but not yet fully assessed.

### Assessing

Risk is being analyzed.

### Mitigating

Actions are actively being implemented.

### Monitoring

Controls exist and the risk is being monitored.

### Escalated

Risk requires higher-level attention.

### Accepted

The remaining risk has been consciously accepted.

### Closed

The risk is no longer relevant or has been sufficiently resolved.

### Archived

The risk remains historically relevant but no longer requires active monitoring.

# 19. Risk Triggers

A trigger is an observable condition indicating that risk exposure may be increasing.

* failure rate increases

* costs rise unexpectedly
* security alerts appear
* deadlines slip
* demand falls
* system capacity approaches limits
* important staff become unavailable

Each significant risk should have useful warning indicators where practical.

# 23. Risk Escalation

A risk should be escalated when:

* score increases significantly
* mitigation fails
* impact becomes larger than expected
* ownership is unclear
* the risk affects strategic objectives
* customer impact becomes significant
* financial exposure increases
* security exposure becomes serious
* legal or regulatory exposure appears
* the risk may affect multiple teams

Escalation should happen early enough to influence the outcome.

---

# 24. Critical Risk Principle

Critical risks should not remain buried inside ordinary project discussions.

When a critical risk is identified:

1. Confirm the facts.
2. Assign an owner.
3. Assess immediate exposure.
4. Determine available mitigation.
5. Establish contingency action.
6. Notify the appropriate authority.
7. Record the decision.
8. Monitor until resolved or accepted.

---

# 25. Risk Ownership

The risk owner is accountable for ensuring that the risk remains visible and managed.

The owner should:

* maintain the record
* review the risk
* coordinate mitigation
* identify triggers
* escalate where necessary
* update stakeholders
* recommend closure or acceptance

Risk ownership does not necessarily mean personally performing every mitigation task.

---

# 26. Risk Action Ownership

A mitigation action may have a different owner from the overall risk.

This separation improves accountability.

# 27. Risk and Decision-Making

Significant risks should be considered when making important decisions.

For a major initiative, ask:

* What could go wrong?
* How likely is it?
* What would the impact be?
* Can the risk be reduced?
* What would the mitigation cost?
* What happens if mitigation fails?
* Is the remaining risk acceptable?

Risk analysis should be part of investment decisions.

# 38. Risk Register Quality Standard

A useful risk record should be:

* specific
* understandable
* evidence-based where possible
* owned
* actionable
* current
* measurable where possible

Avoid vague entries such as:

> "Something might go wrong."

Prefer:

> "A single infrastructure provider failure could make the application unavailable for customers."

# 39. Risk Duplication

Before creating a new risk, check whether a similar risk already exists.

Duplicate risks can cause:

* fragmented mitigation
* inconsistent ownership
* multiple conflicting scores
* repeated work

Related risks may be linked rather than duplicated.

* one key employee
* one customer
* one revenue source
* one technology dependency
* one critical integration

Concentration can create systemic vulnerability even when individual risks appear manageable.

# 42. Risk Appetite

Risk appetite describes the level and type of risk the company is willing to accept while pursuing its objectives.

EyesightWorks should generally seek:

* controlled experimentation
* manageable technical risk
* responsible financial risk
* strong security practices
* evidence-backed product risk
* deliberate strategic risk

The company should avoid unnecessary risk that provides little corresponding value.

Formal risk appetite thresholds may be refined as the organization grows.

# 43. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance refers to how much variation or exposure is acceptable within a specific activity.

Examples may include:

* acceptable downtime
* acceptable budget variance
* acceptable defect rate
* acceptable delivery delay
* acceptable service response time

Tolerances should become more specific as the company matures.

# 45. Risk Closure

A risk may be closed when:

* the underlying condition no longer exists
* the risky activity has ended
* mitigation has removed the relevant exposure
* the risk has been transferred
* the risk is no longer material

Closure should be documented.

---

# 46. Risk Reopening

A closed risk may be reopened when new conditions recreate the exposure.

Examples:

* a previously fixed dependency returns
* infrastructure architecture changes
* a new product reintroduces the issue
* market conditions change
* a previous control becomes ineffective

Historical information should be preserved.

# 47. Incident Relationship

A risk is a potential future problem.

An incident is an event that has already happened.

```text id="xjvp5y"
Risk
 ↓
Warning
 ↓
Incident
 ↓
Response
 ↓
Recovery
 ↓
Learning
```

When a risk becomes an incident, incident management and post-incident learning should supplement the Risk Register.

---

# 48. Post-Incident Learning

After a significant incident, ask:

* Was the risk already known?
* Was it recorded?
* Was the likelihood underestimated?
* Was the impact underestimated?
* Did mitigation fail?
* Was ownership clear?
* Was the trigger detected?
* Was the contingency effective?
* What should change?

Lessons should be incorporated into:

* standards
* processes
* architecture
* training
* monitoring
* risk records
* governance decisions

# 53. Risk Register Health Indicators

The Risk Register is healthy when:

* important risks are visible
* risks have owners
* high risks have active mitigation
* risk scores are current
* overdue actions are visible
* risk trends are reviewed
* significant incidents create learning
* leadership understands major exposures
* closed risks remain historically traceable

The Risk Register may be unhealthy when:

* risks are recorded but never reviewed
* owners are missing
* mitigation actions have no deadlines
* scores are never updated
* every issue is marked critical
* significant risks exist only in people's memory
* risks are hidden to avoid escalation
* the register becomes a list with no action

# 58. Operating Principle

> **A risk that is visible can be managed. A risk that is owned can be acted upon.**

**Identify → Assess → Own → Mitigate → Monitor → Escalate → Learn → Improve**
